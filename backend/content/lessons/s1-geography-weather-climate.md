## Weather or climate?

**Weather** is the condition of the atmosphere at a particular place and time. For example: "It is sunny and warm in Kigali this morning." Weather can change within a few hours.

**Climate** is the average weather of a place, measured over a long period, usually **30 years or more**. For example: "Musanze has a cool, wet climate."

An easy way to remember: weather is what you get today; climate is what you expect.

## Elements of weather and how we measure them

We describe the weather using its **elements**. Each element is measured with an instrument at a **weather station**.

| Element | Instrument | Unit |
|---|---|---|
| Temperature | Thermometer (a Six's thermometer records the maximum and minimum) | degrees Celsius (°C) |
| Rainfall | Rain gauge | millimetres (mm) |
| Humidity (water vapour in the air) | Hygrometer (wet-and-dry bulb thermometer) | percent (%) |
| Wind direction | Wind vane | compass points (N, NE, E …) |
| Wind speed | Anemometer | kilometres per hour (km/h) |
| Air pressure | Barometer | millibars (mb) |
| Sunshine | Sunshine recorder | hours |
| Cloud cover | Observing the sky | oktas (eighths of the sky) |

Thermometers and the hygrometer are kept in a **Stevenson screen**: a white wooden box on legs, about 1.2 m above the ground, with slatted sides. The white paint reflects sunlight and the slats let air flow through, so the thermometers measure the temperature of the air in the shade.

A **rain gauge** is placed in an open space, away from trees and buildings, so that no rain is blocked or dripped into it. A **wind vane** points to the direction the wind is coming **from**: an "east wind" blows from the east.

## Calculating with weather data

- **Daily mean temperature** $= \dfrac{\text{maximum} + \text{minimum}}{2}$
- **Daily range of temperature** $= \text{maximum} - \text{minimum}$
- **Annual rainfall** = the total of the 12 monthly rainfall figures

### Worked example 1: a day in Huye

A school's Six's thermometer in Huye recorded a maximum of 26 °C and a minimum of 14 °C. Find the mean temperature and the range.

1. Mean temperature $= \dfrac{26 + 14}{2} = \dfrac{40}{2} = 20$ °C
2. Daily range $= 26 - 14 = 12$ °C

### Worked example 2: rainfall over a year

The table shows sample monthly rainfall, in mm, for a weather station in central Rwanda.

| Jan | Feb | Mar | Apr | May | Jun | Jul | Aug | Sep | Oct | Nov | Dec |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| 75 | 85 | 120 | 160 | 95 | 20 | 10 | 30 | 60 | 95 | 110 | 80 |

1. Annual total: $75 + 85 + 120 + 160 + 95 + 20 + 10 + 30 + 60 + 95 + 110 + 80 = 940$ mm
2. Wettest month: April (160 mm). Driest month: July (10 mm).
3. March to May: $120 + 160 + 95 = 375$ mm
4. September to December: $60 + 95 + 110 + 80 = 345$ mm
5. June to August: $20 + 10 + 30 = 60$ mm

If you draw these figures as a bar graph, you see **two peaks** of rain in one year. This pattern is typical of Rwanda.

## Rwanda's seasons

Rwanda lies just south of the Equator, between about 1° and 3° south. Near the Equator the sun is overhead twice a year. A belt of rising, moist air that brings heavy rain follows the overhead sun, moving north and then south, so it crosses Rwanda **twice** each year. That is why Rwanda has **two rainy seasons** and **two dry seasons**.

| Season | Kinyarwanda name | Usual months |
|---|---|---|
| Short dry season | Urugaryi | mid-December to February |
| Long rainy season | Itumba | March to May |
| Long dry season | Icyi | June to mid-September |
| Short rainy season | Umuhindo | mid-September to mid-December |

Farmers plan their year around these seasons. Most crops are planted at the start of each rainy season: farming **Season A** begins with the short rains in September, and **Season B** begins with the long rains in March. Beans and maize are harvested and dried in the dry seasons that follow.

## Altitude and temperature

Rwanda is called the "land of a thousand hills". Most of the country lies between about 1,000 m and 2,500 m above sea level, rising to 4,507 m at the top of **Mount Karisimbi**.

Temperature **falls as altitude rises**, by about **6.5 °C for every 1,000 m** (roughly 0.65 °C for every 100 m). This happens because:

- The sun heats the ground, and the ground then heats the air just above it. Air high above the lowlands is further from this source of heat.
- Air that rises **expands** as the pressure around it falls, and expanding air **cools**. Air high up is at lower pressure, so it is cooler.

This is why the lower land of Bugesera and Kirehe in the east (around 1,300 to 1,500 m) is warm, while Musanze and the Nyungwe forest highlands (around 1,800 to 2,900 m) are cool, and the summit of Karisimbi can be close to freezing.

### Worked example 3: up Mount Karisimbi

Kigali is about 1500 m above sea level and the summit of Karisimbi is about 4500 m (both rounded). If it is 24 °C in Kigali, estimate the temperature at the summit.

1. Height difference $= 4500 - 1500 = 3000$ m, which is 3 lots of 1000 m
2. Temperature drop $= 3 \times 6.5 = 19.5$ °C
3. Summit temperature $\approx 24 - 19.5 = 4.5$ °C

## Other factors that affect climate

- **Latitude:** places near the Equator are hot unless they are high up.
- **Large water bodies:** Lake Kivu keeps towns such as Rubavu and Karongi from getting very hot or very cold.
- **Relief:** slopes facing moist winds receive more rain than sheltered slopes.
- **Vegetation:** large forests such as Nyungwe add moisture to the air and help bring rain.

## Safety in weather fieldwork

- Never read instruments or stand in open ground during a **thunderstorm**. Rwanda has frequent lightning, so go indoors and stay away from tall trees, metal poles and water.
- If a glass thermometer breaks, do not touch the glass or the liquid inside. Tell your teacher.
- Never look directly at the sun, even when checking a sunshine recorder.
