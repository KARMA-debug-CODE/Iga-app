## What is history?

**History** is the study of past human activities. Historians are like detectives. They collect clues, called **evidence**, and use them to explain what happened and why.

A **source** is anything that gives us information about the past: an object, a document, a photograph or a story. To find out when your school was built, you could ask the oldest teacher or read the school's old record books. Each of these is a source.

## Primary and secondary sources

- A **primary source** was made at the time you are studying, by people who were there: a letter, a photograph, a tool, an eyewitness account. An **eyewitness** is a person who saw an event with their own eyes.
- A **secondary source** was made later, by someone who studied the past: a textbook, a documentary, an article.

> **Tip:** "old" does not mean "primary". A book written in 1950 about the 1800s is secondary, because its writer was not there.

### Worked example 1: sorting six sources

For each source, ask: "Was it made at the time, by someone who was there?"

| Source | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Your grandmother tells you what she saw on the day electricity reached her village | primary | she is an eyewitness |
| A photograph of a market in Huye, taken in the 1950s | primary | taken at the time |
| Your S1 history textbook | secondary | written later, from other sources |
| A clay pot dug up by archaeologists | primary | made and used at the time |
| A documentary made in 2020 about life in the early 1900s | secondary | made long after |
| A letter written by a colonial official in 1930 | primary | written at the time |

**It depends on your question.** The documentary is secondary for life in the early 1900s. But it is primary if your question is "How did film-makers in 2020 tell the story of the past?" In the same way, when your grandmother repeats what *her* grandmother told her, that part is oral tradition, not her eyewitness account.

## Main sources of history

### Oral tradition

**Oral tradition** is knowledge of the past passed down by word of mouth, from one generation to the next. It includes stories, songs, poems, proverbs and genealogies (family trees). For centuries, before writing was widely used in Rwanda, it was the main way people kept their history alive. It has several kinds, or **genres**:

| Genre | What it is | What it tells a historian |
|---|---|---|
| **Ibitekerezo** | historical stories, some told as long poems, about kings, heroes and great events | which events people remembered, and how they explained them |
| **Ibisigo** | dynastic poetry (poems about the family of kings, the dynasty), made by poets at the king's court to praise the kings | the names and deeds of kings, and what the court valued |
| **Ubucurabwenge** | royal genealogies: the list of kings, recited in order | the order of the kings; counting generations gives rough dates |
| **Imigani** | proverbs and tales told in families | everyday values, beliefs and ways of life |

Some royal texts were learned by heart by specialists at the king's court, which helped keep them accurate. In the 20th century, **Alexis Kagame**, a Rwandan priest and historian, wrote down and studied many of them.

| Strengths | Weaknesses |
|---|---|
| It exists where nothing was written | Details can change each time it is retold |
| It keeps a people's values and culture | Storytellers may exaggerate or favour one side |
| It involves the whole community | Dates are often unclear |
| It is cheap: you only need to listen | Knowledge is lost when elders die |

### Written sources

**Written sources** include books, letters, diaries, newspapers, government records and travellers' accounts. Most written records about Rwanda date from the late 1800s onwards, and many were written by European outsiders.

- **Strengths:** exact dates, names and numbers.
- **Weaknesses:** the writer may be biased or misunderstand what they saw. Only people who could write left records, so many ordinary voices are missing.

### Archaeology

**Archaeology** is the study of material remains, such as tools, pottery, bones and buildings, dug from the ground. Deeper layers are usually older.

Scientists often find the age of remains by **carbon dating**. Living things take in a tiny amount of **carbon-14**, a special kind of carbon atom (an **isotope**) that you meet in the S1 chemistry lesson "Inside the atom". After death, the carbon-14 slowly disappears at a known rate (about half is gone after about 5,700 years), so measuring what is left shows roughly when the plant or animal died. It only works for things that were once alive, like wood, charcoal and bone. The clay of a pot cannot be carbon-dated, because clay was never alive. But charcoal found beside the pot can be dated.

- **Strengths:** evidence from times before writing.
- **Weaknesses:** slow and expensive; objects do not explain themselves; wood and cloth rot away.

### Other sources

- **Museums**, such as the Ethnographic Museum in Huye and the King's Palace Museum in Nyanza.
- **Historical sites**, where events happened.
- **Linguistics**, the study of how languages are related. Kinyarwanda and Kirundi are very close, which suggests a long shared history.
- **Photographs, films and the internet.** Anyone can post online, so check who wrote it.

## Is the source reliable?

A source is **reliable** if we can trust what it tells us. Ask four questions:

1. **Who** made it, and were they there?
2. **When** was it made: soon after the event, or years later?
3. **Why** was it made: to inform, to persuade or to entertain?
4. Does it **agree** with other sources?

When several **independent** sources agree (sources made separately, not copied from each other), we call it **corroboration**. It gives a more reliable picture than one source alone.

### Worked example 2: an invented traveller's account

This account was invented for practice. It is not a real document.

> "Written in 1905 by a European traveller after two days in a hilly region: The people here have no markets and grow very little. They seem to have no chiefs. My government should send officials to bring order."

1. **Who?** An outsider who stayed two days and probably did not speak the language. He may have misunderstood a lot.
2. **When?** 1905, at the time, so it is primary. But the visit was very short.
3. **Why?** The last sentence shows his aim: to persuade his government to take control of the region and rule it as a **colony**. So he may make the people look disorganised.
4. **Agree?** Compare it with oral traditions about local chiefs, farming tools found by archaeologists and other records of markets. If they disagree, trust him less.
5. **Conclusion:** weak evidence about markets and chiefs, but useful evidence about how some Europeans argued for colonial rule.

Check: all four questions answered ✓

### Worked example 3: when did the market begin?

This example is invented. A student wants to know when her village market started. She finds three sources.

- **A:** an elder, aged 80, interviewed in 2024: "The market started the year I got married. I was about 21."
- **B:** the old sector record book shows market fees first collected in 1966.
- **C:** a 2015 newspaper article says the market is "more than 70 years old".

1. Turn each into a date. A: born about $2024 - 80 = 1944$, married about $1944 + 21 = 1965$. B: 1966. C: before $2015 - 70 = 1945$.
2. A and B are one year apart: perhaps the market started in 1965 and fees began the next year.
3. C is about 20 years earlier. It was written much later and does not say where its number came from.
4. **Conclusion:** the market most likely began around 1965–1966.

Check: two independent primary sources agree, so the answer is corroborated ✓

## Bias, fact and opinion

A **point of view** is the position from which a person sees an event. **Bias** is when a source is one-sided: it favours one side, leaves out facts or exaggerates.

Two schools in Rubavu play football and the score is 2–1. The winners write: "We played brilliantly." The losers write: "The referee was unfair." Both agree on the score, but each shows its own point of view. A biased source is still useful, because it shows what that side thought.

A **fact** can be checked and proved true or false. An **opinion** is what someone thinks or feels. Words like *best*, *better*, *beautiful*, *should* and *I think* often signal an opinion.

### Worked example 4: spot the opinion

1. "Rwanda became independent on 1 July 1962." **Fact**: it can be checked in records.
2. "Life in the old days was better than today." **Opinion**: "better" is a judgement.
3. "The Ethnographic Museum is in Huye." **Fact**: you can go and see it.
4. "Oral tradition was passed down by word of mouth." **Fact**.
5. "Proverbs are the most beautiful part of our culture." **Opinion**: people can disagree.

Check: 3 facts and 2 opinions; each opinion has a judgement word ✓

## Try it at home: interview an elder

1. **Ask permission.** Explain it is for your history lesson, and ask if you may take notes or record on a phone.
2. **Prepare questions**, for example:
   - What do you remember about school, the market or farming when you were my age?
   - How did people travel then? What did things cost?
   - Did you see this yourself, or did someone tell you?
   - When did it happen? What else happened that year?
3. **Listen without interrupting.** If a memory is painful, or the person does not want to answer, respect that and move on.
4. **Thank them**, then write down the date, the place and what they said.
5. **Corroborate:** ask a second person the same question.

Never share a recording without the person's permission.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking primary means true.** Primary sources can be biased.
- **Thinking old means primary.** A 1950 book about the 1800s is secondary.
- **Using only one source.** Always look for corroboration.
- **Mixing up fact and opinion.** Look for judgement words.
- **Thinking carbon dating works on anything.** Only on things that were once alive.
