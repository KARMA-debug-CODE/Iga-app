## What is history?

**History** is the study of past human activities. Historians are like detectives. They collect clues, called **evidence**, and use them to explain what happened and why.

A **source** is anything that gives us information about the past. It can be an object, a document, a photograph or a story.

Imagine you want to know when your school was built. You could ask the oldest teacher. You could look for a date on the foundation stone. You could read the school's old record books. Each of these is a source.

## Primary and secondary sources

- A **primary source** was made at the time you are studying, by people who were there. Examples: a letter, a photograph, a tool, an eyewitness account.
- A **secondary source** was made later, by someone who studied the past. Examples: a textbook, a documentary, a magazine article.

An **eyewitness** is a person who saw an event with their own eyes.

> **Tip:** "old" does not mean "primary". A book written in 1950 about the 1800s is a secondary source, because its writer was not there.

### Worked example 1: sorting six sources

Sort each source into primary or secondary. For each one, ask: "Was it made at the time, by someone who was there?"

| Source | Type | Reason |
|---|---|---|
| Your grandmother tells you what she saw on the day electricity reached her village | primary | she is an eyewitness |
| A photograph of a market in Huye, taken in the 1950s | primary | it was taken at the time |
| Your S1 history textbook | secondary | it was written later, using other sources |
| A clay pot dug up by archaeologists | primary | it was made and used by people at the time |
| A documentary made in 2020 about life in the 1900s | secondary | it was made long after the events |
| A letter written by a colonial official in 1930 | primary | he wrote it at the time, about what he saw |

**It depends on your question.** The 2020 documentary is secondary for life in the 1900s. But it is primary if your question is "How did film-makers in 2020 tell the story of the past?"

The same care applies to your grandmother. When she tells you what her own grandmother told her, that part is not her eyewitness account. It is oral tradition.

## Main sources of history

### Oral tradition

**Oral tradition** is knowledge of the past passed down by word of mouth, from one generation to the next. For centuries, before writing was widely used in Rwanda, it was the main way people kept their history alive.

Rwandan oral tradition has several kinds, or **genres**:

| Genre | What it is | What it tells a historian |
|---|---|---|
| **Ibitekerezo** | historical stories, some told as long poems, about kings, heroes and great events | which events people remembered, and how they explained them |
| **Ibisigo** | dynastic poetry, made by court poets to praise the kings | the names and deeds of kings, and what the royal court valued |
| **Ubucurabwenge** | royal genealogies: the list of kings, recited in order | the order of the kings; by counting generations, historians can roughly estimate dates |
| **Imigani** | proverbs and tales told in families | everyday values, beliefs and ways of life |

Some royal texts were learned by heart by specialists at the king's court, which helped keep them accurate. In the 20th century, **Alexis Kagame**, a Rwandan priest and historian, wrote down and studied many of them.

| Strengths of oral tradition | Weaknesses of oral tradition |
|---|---|
| It exists where nothing was written | Details can change each time a story is retold |
| It keeps the values and culture of a people | Storytellers may exaggerate or praise one side (bias) |
| It involves the whole community | Dates are often unclear |
| It is cheap: you only need to listen | Knowledge is lost when elders die without passing it on |

### Written sources

Written sources include books, letters, diaries, newspapers, government records and travellers' accounts. Most written records about Rwanda date from the late 1800s onwards. Many were written by European travellers, missionaries and colonial officials, who saw the country as outsiders.

- **Strengths:** they can give exact dates, names and numbers.
- **Weaknesses:** the writer may be biased or may misunderstand what they saw. Only people who could write left records, so many ordinary voices are missing.

### Archaeology

**Archaeology** is the study of material remains: tools, pottery, bones and buildings dug from the ground. Archaeologists dig slowly and record the layer where each object lies. Deeper layers are usually older.

To find the age of remains, scientists often use **carbon dating**. Living things take in carbon, including a tiny amount of a kind called **carbon-14**. You meet carbon-14 as an **isotope** in the S1 chemistry lesson "Inside the atom". After death, no new carbon-14 comes in, and the carbon-14 already there slowly disappears at a known rate. About half is gone after about 5,700 years. By measuring how much is left, scientists work out roughly when the plant or animal died.

Carbon dating only works for things that were once alive, such as wood, charcoal, bone and seeds. A clay pot cannot be carbon-dated itself, but charcoal found in the same layer can.

- **Strengths:** it gives evidence from times before writing.
- **Weaknesses:** it is slow and expensive; objects do not explain themselves; many things, like wood and cloth, rot away.

### Other sources

- **Museums**, such as the Ethnographic Museum in Huye and the King's Palace Museum in Nyanza.
- **Historical sites**, where you can see where events happened.
- **Linguistics**, the study of how languages are related. Kinyarwanda and Kirundi are very close, which suggests that their speakers share a long history.
- **Photographs, films and the internet.** Anyone can post on the internet, so always check who wrote it.

## Is the source reliable?

A source is **reliable** if we can trust what it tells us. Ask these four questions about any source:

1. **Who** made it, and were they there?
2. **When** was it made? Soon after the event, or many years later?
3. **Why** was it made: to inform, to persuade, or to entertain?
4. Does it **agree** with other sources?

When several independent sources agree, we call it **corroboration**. It gives a much more reliable picture than one source alone.

### Worked example 2: an invented traveller's account

This account was invented for practice. It is not a real document.

> "Notes of a European traveller, written in 1905 after two days in a hilly region: The people here have no markets and grow very little. They do not seem to have any chiefs. My government should send officials to bring order."

1. **Who?** An outsider who stayed only two days. He probably did not speak the local language, so he may have misunderstood a lot.
2. **When?** 1905, at the time of his visit, so it is a primary source. But two days is a very short visit.
3. **Why?** The last sentence shows his aim: to persuade his government to take control. A writer with that aim may make the people look disorganised.
4. **Agree?** Compare it with oral traditions about local chiefs, with farming tools found by archaeologists, and with other records of markets. If they disagree with him, trust him less.
5. **Conclusion:** the account is weak evidence about markets and chiefs. It is useful evidence about what some Europeans thought, and how some of them argued for colonial rule.

Check: all four questions answered, and the conclusion says what the source is useful for ✓

### Worked example 3: when did the market begin?

This example is invented. A student asks: "When did the market in our village start?" She finds three sources.

- **Source A:** an elder, aged 80, interviewed in 2024: "The market started the year I got married. I was about 21."
- **Source B:** the old sector record book shows market fees first collected in 1966.
- **Source C:** a newspaper article from 2015 says the market is "more than 70 years old".

1. Turn each source into a date. A: born about $2024 - 80 = 1944$, married about $1944 + 21 = 1965$. B: 1966. C: before $2015 - 70 = 1945$.
2. Which agree? A and B are only one year apart. Perhaps the market started in 1965 and fees began the next year.
3. Which disagree? C is about 20 years earlier.
4. Judge them. A is an eyewitness. B was written at the time. C was written much later, and does not say where its number came from.
5. **Conclusion:** the market most likely began around 1965–1966.

Check: two independent primary sources agree, so the answer is corroborated ✓

## Point of view, bias, fact and opinion

A **point of view** is the position from which a person sees an event. **Bias** is when a source is one-sided: it favours one side, leaves out facts or exaggerates.

Think of a football match between two schools in Rubavu that ends 2–1. The winners write: "We played brilliantly and deserved to win." The losers write: "The referee was unfair. We were the better team." Both reports agree on the score, but each shows its own point of view.

A biased source is still useful: it tells us what that side thought. Just do not take it as the whole truth.

A **fact** can be checked and proved true or false. An **opinion** is what someone thinks or feels, and it cannot be proved. Words like *best*, *better*, *beautiful*, *should* and *I think* often signal an opinion.

### Worked example 4: spot the opinion

Decide whether each sentence is a fact or an opinion.

1. "Rwanda became independent on 1 July 1962." **Fact**: it can be checked in records.
2. "Life in the old days was better than life today." **Opinion**: "better" is a judgement.
3. "The Ethnographic Museum is in Huye." **Fact**: you can go and see it.
4. "Oral tradition was passed down by word of mouth." **Fact**: elders and records confirm it.
5. "Proverbs are the most beautiful part of our culture." **Opinion**: people can disagree.

Check: 3 facts and 2 opinions, and each opinion contains a judgement word ✓

## Try it at home: interview an elder

You can collect oral history yourself.

1. **Ask permission first.** Explain that it is for your history lesson. Ask if you may take notes or record on a phone.
2. **Prepare your questions**, for example:
   - Where did you grow up? (only if they are happy to say)
   - What do you remember about school, the market or farming when you were my age?
   - How did people travel then? What did things cost?
   - Did you see this yourself, or did someone tell you?
   - When did it happen? What else happened that year?
3. **Listen without interrupting.** If a memory is painful, or the person does not want to answer, respect that and move on.
4. **Thank them.** Then write down the date, the place and what they said.
5. **Corroborate.** Ask a second person the same question, or compare with your textbook.

Never share a recording without the person's permission.

## Common mistakes

- **Thinking primary means true.** A primary source can be biased. A good secondary source may be more reliable because it compares many sources.
- **Thinking old means primary.** A 1950 book about the 1800s is secondary.
- **Believing everything in oral tradition, or rejecting all of it.** Check it against other sources.
- **Using only one source.** Always look for corroboration.
- **Mixing up fact and opinion.** Look for judgement words.
- **Thinking carbon dating works on anything.** It works only on things that were once alive.
