## Needs and wants

- **Needs** are things you must have to live and study: food, shelter (a place to live), school fees, basic clothing, exercise books.
- **Wants** are things that are nice to have, but you can live without them: a new phone, snacks, fashionable shoes.

Ask yourself: "What happens if I do not buy this?" If you cannot live or study well without it, it is a need. If life goes on fine, it is a want.

## Choices and opportunity cost

Money is limited. You cannot buy everything you want, so you must make **choices**.

When you choose one thing, you give up something else. The **opportunity cost** of a choice is the **next-best thing you give up**.

### Worked example 1: Keza's 2,000 FRW

Keza has 2,000 FRW. She would like a data bundle (2,000 FRW) and she also needs two exercise books (1,000 FRW each). She cannot have both.

1. List the choices: a data bundle for 2,000 FRW, or two exercise books for $2 \times 1\,000 = 2\,000$ FRW.
2. Rank them. The books help her study, so they come first. The data bundle is her next-best choice.
3. Keza buys the books.
4. Her opportunity cost is the **data bundle**, the thing she gave up.
5. Check: the opportunity cost is a *thing* (the data bundle), not "2,000 FRW" ✓.

Opportunity cost is not only about money. If you spend Saturday afternoon selling eggs, the opportunity cost might be the football match you missed.

## What is a budget?

A **budget** is a plan for how you will use your money over a period of time (a week, a month or a school term). It has three parts:

- **Income** is the money you receive, for example pocket money or money from selling things.
- **Expenses** are the money you spend.
- **Savings** are the money you keep for later.

$$\text{Income} - \text{Expenses} = \text{Savings (or deficit)}$$

If expenses are bigger than income, nothing is left to save. The missing amount is called a **deficit**.

There are three kinds of budget:

| Kind of budget | What it means | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Surplus** | income is bigger than expenses | income 10,000, expenses 8,000: surplus of 2,000 FRW |
| **Balanced** | income and expenses are equal | income 10,000, expenses 10,000: nothing left |
| **Deficit** | expenses are bigger than income | income 10,000, expenses 11,000: deficit of 1,000 FRW |

A surplus is good: you can save it. A deficit is a warning: you are spending money you do not have.

## Pay yourself first

**Pay yourself first** means: as soon as you receive money, put your savings aside **before** you spend on wants. Then pay for your needs. Then share what is left among your wants.

Why? If you wait to save "what is left", there is usually nothing left.

A good habit is to save at least **10%** of your income. To find 10%, divide by 10. For example, 10% of 9,000 FRW is $9\,000 \div 10 = 900$ FRW.

## Steps to make a budget

1. List all your **income** and add it up.
2. **Pay yourself first**: set your savings aside.
3. List your **needs** and their costs.
4. Share what is left among your **wants**.
5. If your expenses are bigger than your income, **cut wants first**.
6. **Track** what you really spend, and adjust next month.

### Worked example 2: Keza's monthly budget

Keza gets 9,000 FRW pocket money each month. She also sells eggs from her family's hens for 3,000 FRW a month. Let us build her budget step by step.

1. **Income:** $9\,000 + 3\,000 = 12\,000$ FRW.
2. **Save first:** 10% of 12,000 is $12\,000 \div 10 = 1\,200$ FRW. Money left: $12\,000 - 1\,200 = 10\,800$ FRW.
3. **Needs:** transport 4,000 + exercise books 2,000 + soap 1,500 = 7,500 FRW. Money left: $10\,800 - 7\,500 = 3\,300$ FRW.
4. **Wants:** she chooses airtime 2,000 + snacks 1,000 = 3,000 FRW. Money left: $3\,300 - 3\,000 = 300$ FRW.
5. **Leftover:** she adds the 300 FRW to her savings. Total savings: $1\,200 + 300 = 1\,500$ FRW.

Here is her finished budget:

| Item | Type | Amount (FRW) |
|---|---|---|
| Pocket money | income | 9,000 |
| Selling eggs | income | 3,000 |
| **Total income** | | **12,000** |
| Transport | need | 4,000 |
| Exercise books | need | 2,000 |
| Soap | need | 1,500 |
| Airtime | want | 2,000 |
| Snacks | want | 1,000 |
| **Total expenses** | | **10,500** |
| **Savings** | | **1,500** |

Check: $12\,000 - 10\,500 = 1\,500$ FRW ✓. This is a surplus budget, and all the surplus goes into savings.

## When expenses are bigger than income

If you plan to spend more than you receive, you have a deficit. Do not stop paying for needs, and do not rush to borrow. Borrowed money must be paid back, so next month you would have even less. **Cut wants first.**

### Worked example 3: Eric's deficit

Eric's income is 12,000 FRW a month. He plans to spend:

- Needs: transport 4,000 + exercise books 2,000 + soap 1,500 = 7,500 FRW.
- Wants: airtime 3,000 + snacks 2,000 + new earphones 1,000 = 6,000 FRW.

1. Total expenses: $7\,500 + 6\,000 = 13\,500$ FRW.
2. Compare with income: $13\,500 - 12\,000 = 1\,500$ FRW. Expenses are bigger, so this is a **deficit** of 1,500 FRW.
3. Keep the needs. They stay at 7,500 FRW.
4. Cut a want: the earphones can wait. New total: $13\,500 - 1\,000 = 12\,500$ FRW. There is still a deficit of 500 FRW.
5. Cut another want: snacks go from 2,000 to 1,000 FRW. New total: $12\,500 - 1\,000 = 11\,500$ FRW.
6. Now $12\,000 - 11\,500 = 500$ FRW. Eric has a surplus of 500 FRW, which he saves.
7. Check: $7\,500 + 3\,000 + 1\,000 = 11\,500$ FRW ✓.

Next month, Eric can pay himself first, as Keza does.

## Why save?

- For **emergencies**, such as a lost pen or a sudden trip to the health centre.
- To reach a **goal**, such as a bicycle or a football.
- Savings can become **capital**: the money you need to start a small business.

### Worked example 4: Saving for a bicycle

Keza wants a bicycle that costs 60,000 FRW. How long will it take to save for it?

1. Use: number of months = goal ÷ money saved each month.
2. If she saves 4,000 FRW a month: $60\,000 \div 4\,000 = 15$ months.
3. If she saves 5,000 FRW a month: $60\,000 \div 5\,000 = 12$ months.
4. Saving 1,000 FRW more each month gets her the bicycle 3 months sooner.
5. Check: $12 \times 5\,000 = 60\,000$ FRW ✓.

## Ways to save in Rwanda

- **A money box at home.** It is easy, but money can be lost or stolen, and it is tempting to spend it.
- **Ikimina** (plural **ibimina**). A savings group of friends, neighbours or classmates. Each member puts in the same amount every week or month. The group may give the money to members in turns, or lend it to members.
- **Umurenge SACCO.** A **SACCO** is a savings and credit cooperative: its members own it, save in it and can borrow from it. Umurenge SACCOs work at sector (*umurenge*) level, so people can save close to home.
- **A bank account.** Banks keep money safe. A young person usually opens one with a parent or guardian.
- **Mobile money savings.** Some mobile money services have a savings option, kept apart from the money you spend.
- **Ejo Heza.** A long-term savings scheme run by RSSB (the Rwanda Social Security Board). It lets people save small amounts for their future. Ask an adult in your family about it.

## Track your spending

A budget is a plan. **Tracking** means writing down what you really spend, every day. Copy these four columns into your exercise book: Day, What I bought, Need or want, Amount (FRW).

### Worked example 5: What Keza's tracker shows

Here is one week of Keza's tracker:

| Day | What I bought | Need or want | Amount (FRW) |
|---|---|---|---|
| Monday | bus fare | need | 500 |
| Monday | sweets | want | 100 |
| Tuesday | sweets | want | 100 |
| Wednesday | airtime | want | 500 |
| Thursday | bus fare | need | 500 |
| Thursday | sweets | want | 100 |
| Friday | pen | need | 200 |
| Saturday | chapati | want | 300 |

1. Add the needs: $500 + 500 + 200 = 1\,200$ FRW.
2. Add the wants: $100 + 100 + 500 + 100 + 300 = 1\,100$ FRW.
3. Total for the week: $1\,200 + 1\,100 = 2\,300$ FRW.
4. Look for small things that repeat: sweets, 3 times at 100 FRW, so 300 FRW a week.
5. In 4 weeks: $300 \times 4 = 1\,200$ FRW. That is as much as her whole "save first" amount!
6. Compare with her plan in Worked example 2: snacks were planned at 1,000 FRW a month. This week, sweets and chapati cost $300 + 300 = 600$ FRW, so in 4 weeks about $600 \times 4 = 2\,400$ FRW. She is spending more than she planned on snacks, so next week she can cut the sweets.
7. Check: add all eight amounts in the table: $500 + 100 + 100 + 500 + 500 + 100 + 200 + 300 = 2\,300$ FRW ✓.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting small daily spending.** 100 FRW of sweets every school day is $100 \times 5 \times 4 = 2\,000$ FRW a month.
- **Counting a loan as income.** Borrowed money is not yours. You must pay it back.
- **Saving only "what is left".** Usually nothing is left. Pay yourself first.
- **Calling a want a need.** New shoes are a want if your old shoes are still fine.
- **Making a budget and never checking it.** Track your spending and adjust.
