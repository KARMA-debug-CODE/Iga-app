## Counting by weighing

A trader at Kimironko market does not count grains of rice one by one. She weighs them. Chemists have the same problem with atoms: they are far too small and too many to count, so we count them by **weighing** large groups called **moles**.

## Relative atomic mass and relative formula mass

The **relative atomic mass** ($A_r$) of an element is the average mass of its atoms compared with $\frac{1}{12}$ of the mass of a carbon-12 atom. It has **no units**.

| Element | H | C | N | O | Na | Mg | S | Cl | Ca |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| $A_r$ | 1 | 12 | 14 | 16 | 23 | 24 | 32 | 35.5 | 40 |

Chlorine has $A_r = 35.5$ because natural chlorine is a mixture of two isotopes, chlorine-35 and chlorine-37.

The **relative formula mass** ($M_r$) is the sum of the $A_r$ values of all the atoms in a formula. For substances made of molecules it is also called the relative molecular mass.

- $M_r(\text{H}_2\text{O}) = 2(1) + 16 = 18$
- $M_r(\text{CO}_2) = 12 + 2(16) = 44$
- $M_r(\text{Ca(OH)}_2) = 40 + 2(16 + 1) = 40 + 34 = 74$ (everything inside the bracket is multiplied by 2)

## The mole and the Avogadro constant

One **mole** (symbol: mol) is the amount of substance that contains $6.02 \times 10^{23}$ particles. This number is called the **Avogadro constant**, $N_A$. The particles may be atoms, molecules or ions, so always say which.

$$N = n \times N_A$$

where $N$ is the number of particles and $n$ is the amount in moles.

## Molar mass

The **molar mass** ($M$) is the mass of one mole of a substance, in **g/mol**. It has the same number as $A_r$ or $M_r$. One mole of carbon atoms has a mass of 12 g; one mole of water molecules has a mass of 18 g.

$$n = \frac{m}{M} \qquad m = n \times M$$

where $m$ is the mass in grams.

## Molar volume of gases

One mole of **any** gas takes up the same volume at the same temperature and pressure. At room temperature and pressure (**r.t.p.**, about 20 °C and 1 atmosphere) this is **24 dm³** (24 000 cm³).

$$V = n \times 24\ \text{dm}^3$$

At standard temperature and pressure (s.t.p., 0 °C and 1 atmosphere) the molar volume is 22.4 dm³.

| To find… | Use |
|---|---|
| moles from mass | $n = \dfrac{m}{M}$ |
| mass from moles | $m = n \times M$ |
| number of particles | $N = n \times N_A$ |
| volume of a gas at r.t.p. | $V = n \times 24\ \text{dm}^3$ |

## Worked example 1: agricultural lime

Farmers in Nyamagabe spread agricultural lime (calcium carbonate, CaCO₃) on acidic soils. A student weighs out 25 g of CaCO₃.

**(a) Molar mass:** $M_r = 40 + 12 + 3(16) = 40 + 12 + 48 = 100$, so $M = 100$ g/mol.

**(b) Moles:** $n = \dfrac{m}{M} = \dfrac{25}{100} = 0.25$ mol

**(c) Number of formula units:** $N = 0.25 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 1.505 \times 10^{23} \approx 1.51 \times 10^{23}$

## Worked example 2: methane from Lake Kivu

Methane gas (CH₄) is extracted from deep water in Lake Kivu and burned to generate electricity. What volume does 8.0 g of methane occupy at r.t.p., and how many molecules does it contain?

- $M(\text{CH}_4) = 12 + 4(1) = 16$ g/mol
- $n = \dfrac{8.0}{16} = 0.50$ mol
- $V = 0.50 \times 24 = 12$ dm³
- $N = 0.50 \times 6.02 \times 10^{23} = 3.01 \times 10^{23}$ molecules

## Worked example 3: nitrogen in urea fertiliser

Urea, CO(NH₂)₂, is a common nitrogen fertiliser for maize and rice. What percentage of its mass is nitrogen, and how much nitrogen is in a 50 kg bag?

- $M_r = 12 + 16 + 2(14 + 2 \times 1) = 28 + 2(16) = 28 + 32 = 60$
- Mass of nitrogen in one mole $= 2 \times 14 = 28$ g
- Percentage of nitrogen $= \dfrac{28}{60} \times 100 = 46.7\%$
- Nitrogen in the bag $= 50 \times \dfrac{28}{60} = 23.3$ kg

If such a bag costs 40 000 FRW, the farmer pays $40\,000 \div 23.3 \approx 1\,700$ FRW (to 2 significant figures) for each kilogram of nitrogen. Chemists use this kind of calculation to compare fertilisers fairly.

## Moles in equations

A balanced equation tells us the ratio in which **moles** react. When limestone is heated strongly in a lime kiln:

$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) \rightarrow \text{CaO}(s) + \text{CO}_2(g)$$

1 mol of CaCO₃ gives 1 mol of CaO and 1 mol of CO₂. So if 50 g of CaCO₃ is heated:

- $n(\text{CaCO}_3) = 50 \div 100 = 0.50$ mol
- $n(\text{CaO}) = 0.50$ mol, so mass $= 0.50 \times 56 = 28$ g
- $n(\text{CO}_2) = 0.50$ mol, so volume $= 0.50 \times 24 = 12$ dm³ at r.t.p.

Check: the mass of CO₂ is $0.50 \times 44 = 22$ g, and $28 + 22 = 50$ g, so mass is conserved.

## Safety in the lab

When weighing chemicals, use a clean spatula and never touch or taste them. Wipe up spills at once and wash your hands afterwards. Quicklime (CaO) made by heating limestone is corrosive and becomes very hot when water is added, so wear goggles and do not handle it with bare hands.
