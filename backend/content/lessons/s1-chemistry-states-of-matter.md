## What is matter?

**Matter** is anything that has mass and takes up space. The water in Lake Kivu, the air you breathe and the bricks of your classroom are all matter.

Matter is found in three common **states**: **solid**, **liquid** and **gas**. The same substance can exist in all three. Water is ice (solid), water (liquid) and steam or water vapour (gas).

## The particle model

All matter is made of tiny **particles** (atoms, molecules or ions) that are far too small to see. The **particle model** (also called the kinetic theory of matter) says:

- particles are always moving;
- there are forces of attraction between particles;
- the hotter a substance is, the faster its particles move.

| Property | Solid | Liquid | Gas |
|---|---|---|---|
| Arrangement | Packed closely in a regular pattern | Close together, no regular pattern | Far apart, random |
| Movement | Vibrate about fixed positions | Slide past each other | Move quickly in all directions |
| Forces between particles | Very strong | Fairly strong | Very weak |
| Shape | Fixed | Takes the shape of its container | Fills the whole container |
| Volume | Fixed | Fixed | Fills the whole container |
| Can it be squeezed (compressed)? | No | Hardly at all | Yes, easily |

The model explains everyday things. A moto-taxi driver can pump a lot of air into a tyre because gas particles have large spaces between them. You cannot squeeze a stone because its particles are already touching.

## Changes of state

Heating gives particles more energy, so they move faster and can overcome the forces holding them together. Cooling takes energy away, so the particles slow down and the forces pull them closer.

| Change | From → to | Energy | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| Melting | solid → liquid | taken in | butter melting in a warm kitchen |
| Freezing | liquid → solid | given out | water turning to ice in a freezer |
| Evaporation and boiling | liquid → gas | taken in | water leaving coffee beans drying in the sun |
| Condensation | gas → liquid | given out | dew on the grass on a cold morning |
| Sublimation | solid → gas | taken in | iodine crystals, moth balls |
| Deposition | gas → solid | given out | frost on Mount Karisimbi |

The **melting point** is the temperature at which a solid turns into a liquid. The **boiling point** is the temperature at which a liquid boils. Pure water melts at $0\ ^\circ\text{C}$ and boils at $100\ ^\circ\text{C}$ at sea level.

### Evaporation is not the same as boiling

- **Evaporation** happens only at the **surface** of a liquid and at **any** temperature. Warmth, wind and a large surface area make it faster. This is why clothes spread out on a line on a sunny, windy day dry quickly.
- **Boiling** happens **throughout** the liquid, with bubbles of gas, at one fixed temperature.

The boiling point depends on air pressure. Kigali is about 1 500 m above sea level, where the air pressure is lower, so water boils at about $95\ ^\circ\text{C}$. Near the top of Mount Karisimbi it boils at about $85\ ^\circ\text{C}$, so beans take much longer to cook.

Scientists also measure temperature in **kelvin** (K):

$$T\ (\text{K}) = T\ (^\circ\text{C}) + 273$$

### The heating curve

If you heat ice steadily and record the temperature every minute, the graph rises, then stays **flat** while the ice melts, rises again, then stays **flat** while the water boils. During a change of state, the energy is used to overcome the forces between particles, not to make them move faster, so the temperature does not rise.

## Worked example 1: which state?

The table gives melting and boiling points. What state is each substance in at room temperature, $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$?

| Substance | Melting point (°C) | Boiling point (°C) |
|---|---|---|
| Ethanol | −114 | 78 |
| Oxygen | −218 | −183 |
| Iron | 1 538 | 2 862 |
| Mercury | −39 | 357 |

**Rule:** below the melting point, the substance is a solid. Between the melting point and the boiling point, it is a liquid. Above the boiling point, it is a gas.

- Ethanol: $-114 < 25 < 78$, so it is a **liquid**.
- Oxygen: $25$ is above its boiling point of $-183$, so it is a **gas**.
- Iron: $25$ is below its melting point of $1538$, so it is a **solid**.
- Mercury: $-39 < 25 < 357$, so it is a **liquid**.

## Worked example 2: boiling water in Kigali

A student in Kigali heats water that starts at $25\ ^\circ\text{C}$. The temperature rises by $5\ ^\circ\text{C}$ every minute until the water boils at $95\ ^\circ\text{C}$.

**(a) How much must the temperature rise?**
$95 - 25 = 70\ ^\circ\text{C}$

**(b) How long does it take to start boiling?**
$70 \div 5 = 14$ minutes

**(c) What is the boiling point in kelvin?**
$95 + 273 = 368\ \text{K}$

If the student keeps heating, the thermometer stays at $95\ ^\circ\text{C}$ until all the water has boiled away. The energy is being used to separate the particles.

## Diffusion

**Diffusion** is the spreading out of particles from where there are many of them to where there are fewer, until they are evenly mixed. You can smell brochettes grilling from far down the street because particles of gas from the meat diffuse through the air. A purple crystal of potassium permanganate dropped into still water slowly colours all of it.

Diffusion is fastest in gases, slower in liquids and extremely slow in solids, because gas particles move fastest and have the most space.

## Safety in the lab

When heating substances, wear safety goggles and tie back long hair. Hold test tubes with a holder and point them away from people. **Never heat a sealed container**: the gas inside expands and can make it burst. Iodine vapour is harmful, so the sublimation of iodine should only be shown by the teacher in a fume cupboard or a well-ventilated room.
