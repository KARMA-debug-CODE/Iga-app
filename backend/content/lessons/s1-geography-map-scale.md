## Why maps need a scale

A map is a reduced drawing of an area. A whole district can fit on one page. The **scale** tells you how much smaller the map is than the real ground. Without a scale, you cannot measure real distances.

## Three ways to show scale

1. **Statement scale**: in words, for example "1 cm represents 1 km".
2. **Representative fraction (RF)**: a ratio with no units. **1 : 50 000** means 1 unit on the map is 50 000 of the *same* units on the ground. So 1 cm on the map is 50 000 cm on the ground, and 1 mm is 50 000 mm. It can also be written as a fraction, $\dfrac{1}{50\,000}$.
3. **Linear (bar) scale**: a line divided like a ruler, with real distances written on it.

```
0        1        2        3 km
├────────┼────────┼────────┤
```

## The unit ladder

You measure a map in **centimetres (cm)**, but real distances are given in **metres (m)** or **kilometres (km)**. Remember:

$$1\ \text{m} = 100\ \text{cm} \qquad 1\ \text{km} = 1\,000\ \text{m} = 100\,000\ \text{cm}$$

```text
cm ──÷ 100──> m ──÷ 1 000──> km
cm <──× 100── m <──× 1 000── km
```

Going to a **bigger** unit, you **divide**. Going to a **smaller** unit, you **multiply**. To go straight from cm to km, divide by 100 000 (five zeros).

Three quick conversions:

- $250\,000$ cm $\div 100 = 2\,500$ m, and $2\,500$ m $\div 1\,000 = 2.5$ km.
- $50\,000$ cm $= 500$ m $= 0.5$ km.
- $3.5$ km $\times 1\,000 = 3\,500$ m, and $3\,500$ m $\times 100 = 350\,000$ cm.

## Converting between scales

### Worked example 1: from RF to a statement

Write 1 : 25 000 and 1 : 250 000 as statement scales.

1. 1 : 25 000 means 1 cm represents $25\,000$ cm.
2. $25\,000 \div 100 = 250$ m, and $250 \div 1\,000 = 0.25$ km.
3. Statement: "1 cm represents 0.25 km" (or 250 m).
4. In the same way, 1 : 250 000 means 1 cm represents $250\,000$ cm $= 2\,500$ m $= 2.5$ km.

Check: $0.25 \times 100\,000 = 25\,000$ ✓

Here are the scales you will meet most often:

| RF | 1 cm on the map represents |
|---|---|
| 1 : 10 000 | 100 m = 0.1 km |
| 1 : 25 000 | 250 m = 0.25 km |
| 1 : 50 000 | 500 m = 0.5 km |
| 1 : 100 000 | 1 km |
| 1 : 250 000 | 2.5 km |
| 1 : 1 000 000 | 10 km |

### Worked example 2: from a statement to an RF

Write "1 cm represents 2 km" and "4 cm represent 1 km" as RFs.

1. Change the ground distance to cm: $2 \times 100\,000 = 200\,000$ cm. So the RF is **1 : 200 000**.
2. For the second, first find what 1 cm represents: $1 \div 4 = 0.25$ km.
3. $0.25 \times 100\,000 = 25\,000$ cm, so the RF is **1 : 25 000**.

Check: $4 \times 25\,000 = 100\,000$ cm $= 1$ km ✓

## Measuring distance

### Worked example 3: from the map to the ground

On the 1 : 50 000 topographic map used in class, a straight path from a school to a health centre measures 8.4 cm. How far is it really?

1. Multiply the map distance by the scale number, 50 000: $8.4 \times 50\,000 = 420\,000$ cm.
2. Change to metres: $420\,000 \div 100 = 4\,200$ m.
3. Change to kilometres: $4\,200 \div 1\,000 = 4.2$ km.

Shortcut: on 1 : 50 000, 1 cm represents 0.5 km, so $8.4 \times 0.5 = 4.2$ km.

Check: both methods give 4.2 km ✓

### Worked example 4: from the ground to the map

Two schools in Gicumbi District are 3.5 km apart. How far apart are they on a 1 : 50 000 map?

This time we go backwards, so we **divide** by the scale.

1. Change to cm: $3.5 \times 100\,000 = 350\,000$ cm.
2. Divide by the scale: $350\,000 \div 50\,000 = 7$ cm.

Check: $7 \times 0.5 = 3.5$ km ✓

### Worked example 5: reading a bar scale with a paper strip

On many maps, the part of the bar scale to the **left of 0** is divided into small parts. On the bar below, the kilometre left of 0 is cut into 4 small parts, so each small part is $1\,000\ \text{m} \div 4 = 250$ m.

```text
  A─────────────────────B
  |                     |
1       0       1       2       3 km
├─┬─┬─┬─┼───────┼───────┼───────┤
```

1. Lay the straight edge of a strip of paper between the two places on the map. Mark the first place A and the second B.
2. Move the strip onto the bar scale. Put mark B on a whole kilometre, so that mark A lands in the small parts left of 0.
3. Read B: it is on 2 km.
4. Read A: it is 3 small parts left of 0, which is $3 \times 250 = 750$ m.
5. Add: 2 km + 750 m = **2.75 km**.

Check: $2 + 3 \times 0.25 = 2.75$ km ✓

You did not need the RF at all: the bar scale did the work.

### Worked example 6: a curved road

A road or river is rarely straight. You can lay a piece of **string** along it, mark the ends, then straighten the string along a ruler or the bar scale. Or you can split the curve into short straight parts and measure each one.

A winding road from a market to a school in Nyamagabe District is measured on a 1 : 50 000 map in four straight parts: 2.3 cm, 1.8 cm, 3.1 cm and 0.9 cm. How long is the road, and how long does it take to walk at 4 km/h?

1. Add the parts: $2.3 + 1.8 + 3.1 + 0.9 = 8.1$ cm.
2. Convert: $8.1 \times 0.5 = 4.05$ km.
3. At 4 km/h you walk 4 km in each hour. So the time is distance ÷ speed: $4.05 \div 4 \approx 1.01$ h, which is **about 1 hour**. (This is the formula speed = distance ÷ time from the S1 physics lesson "Speed, velocity and acceleration", turned around.)

Check: $4 \times 1 = 4$ km, very close to 4.05 km ✓

A ruler laid straight from start to end would give a shorter, wrong answer, because a straight line is the shortest path.

## Zoomed and photocopied maps

When you zoom in on a photo of a map on your phone, or a map is photocopied bigger, the map grows. But the printed RF stays the same, so it is now **wrong**.

Suppose a 1 : 50 000 map is enlarged 2 times. Two villages that were 2 cm apart are now 4 cm apart.

- Using the printed RF: $4 \times 0.5 = 2$ km. Wrong!
- The truth: 4 cm on the copy is 2 cm on the original, and $2 \times 0.5 = 1$ km.

The bar scale grows together with the map, so it stays correct.

> **Tip:** on a zoomed, enlarged or reduced map, always use the bar scale, not the RF. Many map apps on phones show a small bar scale that changes as you zoom.

## Large and small scale

| | Example | Area shown | Detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Large scale** | 1 : 10 000 | small, e.g. a town plan of Musanze | a lot |
| **Small scale** | 1 : 1 000 000 | large, e.g. a map of East Africa | little |

A good way to remember: in 1 : 10 000 the *fraction* $\dfrac{1}{10\,000}$ is **larger** than $\dfrac{1}{1\,000\,000}$. Large fraction, large scale, small area. So the **smaller** the number after the colon, the **larger** the scale and the more detail you see.

## Common mistakes

- **Forgetting to convert.** "420 000" is not an answer. Finish with km or m.
- **Wrong number of zeros.** 50 000 cm is 0.5 km, not 5 km. 1 km = 100 000 cm.
- **Multiplying from ground to map.** From the ground to the map, divide.
- **Measuring a curve with a straight ruler.** Use string or straight parts.
- **Using the RF on a zoomed map.** Use the bar scale.
- **Mixing up large and small scale.** 1 : 10 000 is large scale: more detail, smaller area.
