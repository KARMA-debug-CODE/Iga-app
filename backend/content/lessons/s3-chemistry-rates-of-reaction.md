## How fast is a reaction?

Some reactions are very fast. A match bursts into flame in less than a second. Others are slow: an iron nail left in damp air takes days or weeks to rust, and milk takes a day or two to go sour.

The **rate of reaction** tells us how quickly reactants are used up or products are formed. We usually find an **average rate** over a period of time:

$$\text{average rate} = \frac{\text{change in amount of reactant or product}}{\text{time taken}}$$

The unit depends on what we measure: cm³/s for a volume of gas, g/s or g/min for a change in mass.

## Collision theory

For particles to react they must:

1. **collide** with each other, and
2. collide with at least a minimum amount of energy, called the **activation energy** ($E_a$).

Collisions with less energy than $E_a$ just bounce apart with no reaction. Anything that makes **successful collisions** happen more often makes the reaction faster.

## Factors that affect the rate

| Factor | Change | Effect on rate | Reason |
|---|---|---|---|
| Concentration (pressure for gases) | increase | faster | more particles in the same volume, so they collide more often |
| Temperature | increase | faster | particles move faster, so they collide more often **and** more collisions have energy above $E_a$ |
| Surface area of a solid | break into smaller pieces or powder | faster | more particles are exposed and can be hit |
| Catalyst | add one | faster | gives a different route with a lower activation energy |

A **catalyst** speeds up a reaction without being used up. At the end it is chemically unchanged and has the same mass, so it can be used again. It does **not** change the amount of product, only how quickly it forms. **Enzymes** are biological catalysts.

You see these factors every day in Rwanda:

- Milk lasts longer in a fridge than on a warm kitchen shelf, because low temperature slows down the reactions caused by bacteria. This is why milk from dairy farms in Nyagatare is cooled at milk collection centres before it travels to Kigali.
- Small sticks or small pieces of charcoal catch fire faster than a big log: they have a larger surface area.
- Yeast used to make bread contains enzymes that speed up fermentation.
- Raising the temperature by about 10 °C roughly doubles the rate of many reactions. This is why food goes bad faster in hot weather.

## Measuring the rate

A common school experiment uses marble chips (calcium carbonate) and dilute hydrochloric acid:

$$\text{CaCO}_3(s) + 2\text{HCl}(aq) \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2(aq) + \text{H}_2\text{O}(l) + \text{CO}_2(g)$$

We can follow the reaction in two ways:

- **Collect the gas** in a gas syringe (or an upturned measuring cylinder full of water) and record the volume every 30 seconds.
- **Measure the loss in mass**: put the flask on a balance with a loose cotton-wool plug and record the mass as carbon dioxide escapes.

On a graph of volume of gas against time, the curve is **steepest at the start** (fastest rate), gets less steep, and finally becomes **flat** when the reaction stops. The rate falls because the acid gets used up, so its concentration decreases and collisions become less frequent.

## Worked example 1: reading gas volume data

A student added marble chips to dilute hydrochloric acid and collected the carbon dioxide.

| Time (s) | 0 | 30 | 60 | 90 | 120 | 150 | 180 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Volume of CO₂ (cm³) | 0 | 30 | 52 | 68 | 78 | 82 | 82 |

**(a) Average rate in the first 30 s**

$$\text{rate} = \frac{30 - 0}{30 - 0} = \frac{30}{30} = 1.0\ \text{cm}^3/\text{s}$$

**(b) Average rate between 60 s and 90 s**

$$\text{rate} = \frac{68 - 52}{90 - 60} = \frac{16}{30} \approx 0.53\ \text{cm}^3/\text{s}$$

The rate is lower later on because the acid is less concentrated.

**(c) When had the reaction finished?** The volume is 82 cm³ at both 150 s and 180 s, so no more gas was made after 150 s. The reaction had finished by 150 s.

**(d) Average rate for the whole reaction (first 150 s)**

$$\text{rate} = \frac{82}{150} \approx 0.55\ \text{cm}^3/\text{s}$$

## Worked example 2: loss in mass

A flask containing marble chips and acid stands on a balance. The reading is 152.40 g at the start and 151.52 g after 2 minutes. Find the average rate.

- Mass of CO₂ lost $= 152.40 - 151.52 = 0.88$ g
- Time $= 2$ min $= 2 \times 60 = 120$ s
- Average rate $= \dfrac{0.88}{120} \approx 0.0073$ g/s, which is the same as $\dfrac{0.88}{2} = 0.44$ g/min

Linking to the mole: $0.88$ g of CO₂ is $0.88 \div 44 = 0.020$ mol of carbon dioxide.

## Comparing experiments

If the student repeats the experiment with **powdered** marble of the same mass, the curve is steeper at the start, but it levels off at the **same** final volume (82 cm³), because the same amounts of marble and acid are used, so the same amount of calcium carbonate reacts. Changing the rate does not change how much product is made when the same amounts of reactants are used.

## Safety in the lab

Dilute hydrochloric acid irritates skin and eyes, so wear safety goggles and wash any splashes off with plenty of water. **Never seal the flask** with a tight stopper unless it is connected to a gas syringe: the gas pressure could push the stopper out or break the glass. Take care with hot water baths, and work in a well-ventilated room.
