## What is a market?

A **market** is any place or system where **buyers** and **sellers** exchange a good or service for money, like Kimironko market in Kigali.

Buyers create **demand**. Sellers create **supply**. Together they decide the **price**. We will follow one example: dry beans at Kimironko. The numbers are made up to keep the arithmetic simple.

## Demand

**Demand** is the quantity of a good that buyers are *willing and able* to buy at each possible price, in a given time. *Willing* means you want it. *Able* means you have the money. Wanting a phone with no money does not count.

The **quantity demanded** is the amount wanted at **one** particular price. A **demand schedule** is a table of prices and quantities demanded:

| Price (FRW per kg) | Quantity demanded (kg per day) |
|---|---|
| 600 | 500 |
| 800 | 400 |
| 1,000 | 300 |
| 1,200 | 200 |
| 1,400 | 100 |

The higher the price, the less people buy. This is the **law of demand**: *ceteris paribus*, when the price of a good **rises**, the quantity demanded **falls**.

*Ceteris paribus* is Latin for "other things being equal". We change **only** the price. Incomes, tastes and other prices stay the same.

Plot the schedule on a graph and you get the **demand curve**. Always put **price on the vertical axis** and **quantity on the horizontal axis**. The demand curve slopes **downwards**.

Why? Two reasons:

- **Substitution effect:** when beans become more expensive, some buyers switch to a cheaper food, such as peas.
- **Income effect:** the same money buys less. With 2,400 FRW, a mother gets 3 kg of beans at 800 FRW per kg, but only 2 kg at 1,200 FRW.

## Supply

**Supply** is the quantity sellers are willing and able to offer at each possible price, in a given time. The **quantity supplied** is the amount offered at one price. Here is the **supply schedule**:

| Price (FRW per kg) | Quantity supplied (kg per day) |
|---|---|
| 600 | 100 |
| 800 | 200 |
| 1,000 | 300 |
| 1,200 | 400 |
| 1,400 | 500 |

The **law of supply**: *ceteris paribus*, when the price **rises**, the quantity supplied **rises**. The **supply curve** slopes **upwards**.

Why? Two reasons:

- **Profit:** a higher price means more profit per kilogram, so traders bring out stored beans and fetch beans from farther districts.
- **Cost:** producing more costs more for each extra kilogram (more land, workers, transport). It is only worth it at a higher price.

## Equilibrium

Now put the two curves on one graph. D is demand, S is supply, and they cross at E.

```text
Price (FRW/kg)
 1400 |  D               S
 1200 |      D       S
 1000 |          E
  800 |      S       D
  600 |  S               D
      +--+---+---+---+---+--
        100 200 300 400 500
       Quantity (kg per day)
```

The market is in **equilibrium** where quantity demanded equals quantity supplied. This gives the **equilibrium price** and the **equilibrium quantity**. Here nothing pushes the price up or down.

- Price **above** equilibrium: sellers offer more than buyers want. The extra is a **surplus** (**excess supply**).
- Price **below** equilibrium: buyers want more than sellers offer. The gap is a **shortage** (**excess demand**).

### Worked example 1: Reading the bean market

Find the equilibrium. Then explain what happens at 1,200 FRW and at 800 FRW.

1. Find the price where both schedules give the same quantity: at 1,000 FRW, 300 kg are demanded and 300 kg supplied. Equilibrium: 1,000 FRW per kg, 300 kg per day.
2. At 1,200 FRW: demanded 200 kg, supplied 400 kg. Surplus $= 400 - 200 = 200$ kg. Sellers do not want to carry beans home, so they cut the price. Buyers then buy more and sellers offer less, until the price is back at 1,000 FRW.
3. At 800 FRW: demanded 400 kg, supplied 200 kg. Shortage $= 400 - 200 = 200$ kg. Buyers compete for the few beans, so sellers raise the price back towards 1,000 FRW.
4. Check: at 1,000 FRW the gap is $300 - 300 = 0$. Correct.

### Worked example 2: Equilibrium from equations

The schedules come from $Q_d = 800 - 0.5P$ and $Q_s = -200 + 0.5P$. Here $Q_d$ is the quantity demanded and $Q_s$ the quantity supplied (kg per day), and $P$ is the price (FRW per kg). Find the equilibrium with algebra.

1. At equilibrium $Q_d = Q_s$: $800 - 0.5P = -200 + 0.5P$.
2. Get the $P$ terms on one side and the numbers on the other. Add $0.5P$ to both sides, and add $200$ to both sides: $800 + 200 = 0.5P + 0.5P$.
3. Simplify: half of $P$ plus half of $P$ is one whole $P$, so $1\,000 = P$. The equilibrium price is 1,000 FRW per kg.
4. Demand: $Q_d = 800 - 0.5(1\,000) = 800 - 500 = 300$ kg.
5. Check with supply: $Q_s = -200 + 0.5(1\,000) = -200 + 500 = 300$ kg. Correct. This matches the table.

> **Tip:** always put the price back into **both** equations. If the quantities differ, look for a mistake.

> **Different numbers in front of $P$?** Use the same steps. For $Q_d = 70 - 2P$ and $Q_s = 3P - 10$: $70 - 2P = 3P - 10$. Add $2P$ and $10$ to both sides: $80 = 5P$, so $P = 80 \div 5 = 16$. Then $Q_d = 70 - 2(16) = 38$, and the check gives $Q_s = 3(16) - 10 = 38$, the same.

## What shifts the demand curve?

The law of demand is about the good's **own price**. When **another** factor changes, buyers want a different amount **at every price**, so the whole curve moves. This is a **change in demand**: a shift **right** is an **increase**, a shift **left** is a **decrease**.

- **Income:** higher incomes mean more demand for most goods, such as beans and meat.
- **Substitutes** are goods used *instead of* each other, like beans and peas. If peas become more expensive, demand for beans rises.
- **Complements** are goods used *together*, like tea and sugar, or phones and airtime. If sugar becomes more expensive, people drink less sweet tea, so demand for tea falls.
- **Tastes and fashion:** if a radio programme says beans are healthy, demand rises.
- **Population:** more people in a town means more buyers.
- **Expectations:** if people expect prices to rise next month, they buy more now.

## What shifts the supply curve?

A **change in supply** also moves the whole curve: right is an increase, left is a decrease.

- **Costs of production:** if fertiliser, wages or fuel cost more, supply falls.
- **Technology:** better seeds or machines raise supply.
- **Weather:** good rains raise farm supply; a drought cuts it.
- **Taxes and subsidies:** a tax raises costs and cuts supply. A **subsidy** (government money paid to producers) raises supply.
- **Number of sellers:** more traders, more supply.

## Movement along vs shift

| | Movement along the curve | Shift of the curve |
|---|---|---|
| Cause | the good's **own price** changes | **another factor** changes (income, tastes, related prices...) |
| Name | change in **quantity demanded** | change in **demand** |
| Words | **extension** (more bought) or **contraction** (less bought) | **increase** (right) or **decrease** (left) |
| Graph | a new point on the **same** curve | a **new** curve |

```text
MOVEMENT ALONG one curve
P
|  \
|   A    1,000 FRW: 300 kg
|    \
|     B  800 FRW: 400 kg
|      \ D
+------------ Q
```

```text
SHIFT of the curve
P
|  \       \
|   \       \
|    \ -->   \
|     \       \
|      D1      D2
+---------------- Q
```

Supply works the same way. An own-price change is a movement along the supply curve: a change in **quantity supplied**. Costs, weather or technology shift the whole curve: a change in **supply**.

### Worked example 3: The price of beans rises

The price rises from 800 to 1,000 FRW per kg. Nothing else changes.

1. Only the own price changed, so this is a **movement along** the demand curve, from B to A.
2. Quantity demanded falls from 400 kg to 300 kg: a **contraction** of 100 kg.
3. Check: $Q_d = 800 - 0.5(1\,000) = 300$ kg. Correct. Say "quantity demanded fell", not "demand fell".

### Worked example 4: Incomes rise in Kigali

Higher incomes mean buyers want 100 kg more at every price: $Q_d = 900 - 0.5P$. Supply is unchanged. Find the new equilibrium.

1. Income is not the own price, so demand **shifts right**.
2. Set demand equal to supply: $900 - 0.5P = -200 + 0.5P$. Then $900 + 200 = 0.5P + 0.5P$, so $P = 1\,100$ FRW.
3. Quantity: $Q_d = 900 - 0.5(1\,100) = 900 - 550 = 350$ kg.
4. Check: $Q_s = -200 + 0.5(1\,100) = -200 + 550 = 350$ kg. Correct.
5. Price rises from 1,000 to 1,100 FRW, and quantity rises from 300 to 350 kg. Supply did not shift; sellers moved along it.

### Worked example 5: A drought hits the bean harvest

A drought means sellers offer 100 kg less at every price: $Q_s = -300 + 0.5P$. Demand is $Q_d = 800 - 0.5P$ again. Find the new equilibrium.

1. Weather is not the own price, so supply **shifts left**.
2. Set them equal: $800 - 0.5P = -300 + 0.5P$. Then $800 + 300 = 0.5P + 0.5P$, so $P = 1\,100$ FRW.
3. Quantity: $Q_d = 800 - 0.5(1\,100) = 800 - 550 = 250$ kg.
4. Check: $Q_s = -300 + 0.5(1\,100) = -300 + 550 = 250$ kg. Correct.
5. Price rises from 1,000 to 1,100 FRW, but quantity falls from 300 to 250 kg. Buyers pay more and get less.

## Common mistakes

- **Demand vs quantity demanded.** An own-price change changes *quantity demanded*. Only other factors change *demand*.
- **Drawing a new curve for a price change.** A price change is a movement along the curve.
- **Swapping the axes.** Price goes on the vertical axis.
- **Mixing up surplus and shortage.** Price too high: surplus. Price too low: shortage.
- **Calling a rightward shift of supply a "fall".** It looks lower on the graph, but it is an increase.
