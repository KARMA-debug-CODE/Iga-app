## Forces and motion

A **force** is a push or a pull. It is measured in **newtons (N)** and it is a **vector**, so its direction matters as well as its size.

When several forces act on one object, we add them, taking direction into account. The result is the **resultant force** (also called the **net force**).

- If the forces are **balanced**, the resultant force is zero.
- If the forces are **unbalanced**, there is a resultant force in one direction.

Isaac Newton described how resultant forces affect motion in three laws.

## Newton's first law: inertia

> An object stays at rest, or keeps moving at constant velocity in a straight line, unless a resultant force acts on it.

This tendency of an object to resist any change in its motion is called **inertia**. The greater the **mass**, the greater the inertia.

Everyday examples in Rwanda:

- When a bus from Kigali to Huye brakes suddenly, the passengers lurch **forward**. Their bodies keep moving after the bus slows down. Seat belts give the resultant force needed to stop them safely.
- A passenger on a moto taxi wears a helmet and holds on, because if the moto stops suddenly the passenger keeps moving forward.
- When a bus pulls away quickly, standing passengers fall **backwards**. Their bodies tend to stay at rest.

Note that a car moving at a **steady 60 km/h on a straight, level road** has zero resultant force on it. The engine's driving force exactly balances friction and air resistance.

## Newton's second law: F = ma

> The acceleration of an object is directly proportional to the resultant force on it and inversely proportional to its mass.

$$F = ma$$

where $F$ is the resultant force in N, $m$ is the mass in kg and $a$ is the acceleration in m/s².

This also defines the newton: **1 N is the force that gives a mass of 1 kg an acceleration of 1 m/s²**.

Remember from S1 that acceleration is

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t}$$

where $u$ is the starting velocity and $v$ is the final velocity.

### Weight is a force too

The **weight** of an object is the gravitational force on it:

$$W = mg$$

In this lesson we take $g = 10\ \text{N/kg}$ (more precisely 9.8 N/kg). A 50 kg sack of maize has a weight of $50 \times 10 = 500$ N. Mass stays the same everywhere, but weight would be smaller on the Moon.

### Worked example 1: a moto taxi

A moto taxi with its rider and passenger has a total mass of 250 kg. It starts from rest at a stage in Nyabugogo and reaches 12 m/s in 6 s. Friction and air resistance together are 150 N.

**(a) Acceleration:**

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{12 - 0}{6} = 2\ \text{m/s}^2$$

**(b) Resultant force needed:**

$$F = ma = 250 \times 2 = 500\ \text{N}$$

**(c) Driving force from the engine.** The resultant force is the driving force minus the resistive forces:

$$\text{driving force} - 150 = 500 \quad\Rightarrow\quad \text{driving force} = 650\ \text{N}$$

### Worked example 2: dragging a sack of maize

A farmer in Nyagatare drags a 50 kg sack of maize across a floor with a horizontal force of 120 N. The friction force is 70 N.

**Resultant force:** $120 - 70 = 50$ N (in the direction of the pull).

**Acceleration:**

$$a = \frac{F}{m} = \frac{50}{50} = 1\ \text{m/s}^2$$

### Worked example 3: a truck braking

A loaded truck of mass 3000 kg is travelling at 20 m/s on the road to Rubavu. The driver brakes and it stops in 5 s.

$$a = \frac{v - u}{t} = \frac{0 - 20}{5} = -4\ \text{m/s}^2$$

The minus sign shows a **deceleration**. The braking force has size

$$F = ma = 3000 \times 4 = 12\,000\ \text{N}$$

A heavier truck at the same speed needs a bigger force, or a longer time, to stop. This is why overloaded trucks are dangerous on steep roads.

## Newton's third law: action and reaction

> If object A exerts a force on object B, then object B exerts a force on object A that is equal in size and opposite in direction.

These two forces are called an **action–reaction pair**. They:

- are **equal in size** and **opposite in direction**;
- act on **two different objects**, so they never cancel each other out;
- are the **same type** of force (both contact forces, or both gravitational, and so on).

Examples:

- A fisherman on Lake Kivu pushes the water **backwards** with his paddle; the water pushes the canoe **forwards**.
- When you walk, your foot pushes the ground backwards; the ground pushes you forwards.
- A rocket pushes hot gases downwards; the gases push the rocket upwards.

## Summary table

| Law | Main idea | Key words | Example |
|---|---|---|---|
| First | No resultant force means no change in velocity | inertia, balanced forces | passengers lurch forward when a bus brakes |
| Second | Resultant force causes acceleration, $F = ma$ | resultant force, mass, acceleration | engine force accelerating a moto |
| Third | Forces come in equal and opposite pairs on different objects | action, reaction | a paddle pushing water back |

## Safety in the laboratory

When you investigate $F = ma$ with trolleys, ramps and slotted masses:

- Put a box or a cushion at the end of the runway so trolleys cannot fall off the bench.
- Keep your feet away from hanging masses in case the string breaks.
- Never stand in the path of a moving trolley, and do not overload the pulley.
