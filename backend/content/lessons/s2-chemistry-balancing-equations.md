## Why balance?

A **chemical reaction** makes new substances. The substances you start with are the **reactants**. The new substances are the **products**. A **chemical equation** shows a reaction in symbols. The arrow (→) means "react to make".

In a reaction, atoms are **rearranged**. Old bonds break and the atoms join up in new ways. No atom is created and no atom is destroyed, so the total mass stays the same. This is the **law of conservation of mass**.

Think of a mason who knocks down a small brick wall and builds a bench with the same bricks. The shape changes, but the number of bricks does not.

So a **balanced equation** has the same number of each type of atom on both sides of the arrow.

## Reading a formula: counting atoms

Before you can balance, you must count atoms correctly.

- A symbol on its own means one atom. In H₂O, the O means one oxygen atom.
- A **subscript** is the small number written low, after a symbol. It counts only the atom just before it. H₂O has 2 H atoms and 1 O atom.
- A **coefficient** is the big number in front of a formula. It multiplies every atom in that formula. 3H₂O means three water molecules: 6 H and 3 O.
- A small number after a **bracket** multiplies everything inside the bracket. Ca(OH)₂ has 1 Ca, 2 O and 2 H.

So: atoms of an element = coefficient × subscript (× the number after the bracket, if there is one).

| Formula | Atoms of each element |
|---|---|
| H₂O | H 2, O 1 |
| 3H₂SO₄ | H 3 × 2 = 6, S 3 × 1 = 3, O 3 × 4 = 12 |
| Ca(OH)₂ | Ca 1, O 1 × 2 = 2, H 1 × 2 = 2 |
| 2Ca(OH)₂ | Ca 2, O 2 × 2 = 4, H 2 × 2 = 4 |

Some groups of atoms carry an electric charge and often stay together in a reaction, like one piece. Each group is a **polyatomic ion**. Examples are OH (hydroxide), SO₄ (sulfate), NO₃ (nitrate) and CO₃ (carbonate).

Now try counting a big equation. Green plants, such as the banana and tea plants on our hills, make glucose (C₆H₁₂O₆) by **photosynthesis**:

$$6\text{CO}_2 + 6\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow \text{C}_6\text{H}_{12}\text{O}_6 + 6\text{O}_2$$

- Left: C $6 \times 1 = 6$; H $6 \times 2 = 12$; O $(6 \times 2) + (6 \times 1) = 18$.
- Right: C 6; H 12; O $6 + (6 \times 2) = 18$.

Every element matches, so this equation is balanced ✓

## Word equation → symbol equation

Hydrogen burns in oxygen to make water:

$$\text{hydrogen} + \text{oxygen} \rightarrow \text{water}$$

$$\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O} \quad \text{(not balanced)}$$

Count: the left has 2 O, but the right has only 1 O. One oxygen atom seems to disappear, which is impossible. We must balance it.

## The method

1. Write the correct formula of every reactant and product. **Never change a formula** to balance (H₂O must stay H₂O).
2. Make an **atom-count table**: list each element, then count its atoms on the left and on the right.
3. Choose one element that does not match. Fix it by changing a **coefficient**. Leave elements that appear on their own (like O₂, H₂ or Fe) until last.
4. Recount every element. A new coefficient can change the count of other elements too.
5. Repeat until every row of the table matches. Use the smallest whole numbers.

A coefficient of 1 is not written: CO₂ means 1CO₂.

> **Tip:** when a fuel made of carbon and hydrogen burns, balance **C first, then H, and O last**.

### Worked example 1: water from hydrogen and oxygen

$$\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

1. Count. H: 2 on the left, 2 on the right (matches). O: 2 on the left, 1 on the right (does not match).
2. Fix O: put 2 in front of H₂O. The right now has 2 O, but also $2 \times 2 = 4$ H.
3. Recount H: 2 on the left, 4 on the right. Put 2 in front of H₂. The left now has $2 \times 2 = 4$ H.
4. Final equation:

$$2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Atom | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| H | 2 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| O | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 |

Check: H 4 = 4, O 2 = 2 ✓

### Worked example 2: burning methane from Lake Kivu

Lake Kivu holds methane gas (CH₄) dissolved in its deep water. Some of it is taken out and burned to make electricity. Methane burns in oxygen to make carbon dioxide and water.

$$\text{CH}_4 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

1. C first: 1 on the left, 1 on the right. It matches.
2. H next: 4 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 2 in front of H₂O. The right now has $2 \times 2 = 4$ H.
3. O last: the right has 2 O in CO₂ and $2 \times 1 = 2$ O in 2H₂O, so 4 O. Oxygen comes as O₂ (2 atoms each), so the left needs $4 \div 2 = 2$ O₂.
4. Final equation:

$$\text{CH}_4 + 2\text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Atom | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| H | 4 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| O | 2 | 3 | 4 | 4 |

Check: C 1 = 1, H 4 = 4, O 4 = 4 ✓. You will use this methane again in the lesson on the mole concept.

### Worked example 3: aluminium and oxygen (the LCM trick)

A new aluminium saucepan is shiny, but its surface soon gets a very thin layer of aluminium oxide, Al₂O₃, which protects the metal.

$$\text{Al} + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$$

1. Count. Al: 1 on the left, 2 on the right. O: 2 on the left, 3 on the right.
2. Both reactants are elements, so start with the hard one, oxygen. On the left O comes in 2s; on the right it comes in 3s.
3. Find the **lowest common multiple (LCM)** of 2 and 3: the smallest number that both 2 and 3 divide into. It is 6.
4. Make 6 O on each side: $3\text{O}_2$ gives $3 \times 2 = 6$, and $2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$ gives $2 \times 3 = 6$.
5. Recount Al: the right now has $2 \times 2 = 4$ Al. Put 4 in front of Al.
6. Final equation:

$$4\text{Al} + 3\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 2\text{Al}_2\text{O}_3$$

| Atom | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Al | 1 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| O | 2 | 3 | 6 | 6 |

Check: Al 4 = 4, O 6 = 6 ✓

### Worked example 4: cooking gas

Cooking gas sold in cylinders (LPG) is mainly propane and butane. Propane, C₃H₈, burns in oxygen to make carbon dioxide and water.

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + \text{O}_2 \rightarrow \text{CO}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

1. C first: 3 on the left, 1 on the right. Put 3 in front of CO₂.
2. H next: 8 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 4 in front of H₂O, because $4 \times 2 = 8$.
3. O last: the right has $3 \times 2 = 6$ O in 3CO₂ and $4 \times 1 = 4$ O in 4H₂O. That is $6 + 4 = 10$ O. The left needs $10 \div 2 = 5$ O₂.
4. Final equation:

$$\text{C}_3\text{H}_8 + 5\text{O}_2 \rightarrow 3\text{CO}_2 + 4\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Atom | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| C | 3 | 1 | 3 | 3 |
| H | 8 | 2 | 8 | 8 |
| O | 2 | 3 | 10 | 10 |

Check: C 3 = 3, H 8 = 8, O 10 = 10 ✓

## Equations with brackets and groups

Two rules help with bigger formulas:

- Always multiply out a bracket when you count.
- If a polyatomic ion (like SO₄) appears **unchanged on both sides**, count it as one unit. You do not need to count its S and O atoms separately.

### Worked example 5: lime and acid

Lime is used to make acidic soils less acidic. One kind of lime is slaked lime, calcium hydroxide, Ca(OH)₂. It reacts with hydrochloric acid, HCl, to make calcium chloride and water.

$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2 + \text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

1. Count, multiplying the bracket. Left: Ca 1, O 2, H $2 + 1 = 3$, Cl 1. Right: Ca 1, Cl 2, H 2, O 1.
2. Ca matches.
3. Cl: 1 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 2 in front of HCl. The left now has H $2 + 2 = 4$.
4. H: 4 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 2 in front of H₂O. The right now has 4 H and 2 O.
5. O: 2 on the left, 2 on the right. It matches.
6. Final equation:

$$\text{Ca(OH)}_2 + 2\text{HCl} \rightarrow \text{CaCl}_2 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Atom | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ca | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| Cl | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| H | 3 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| O | 2 | 1 | 2 | 2 |

Check: every row matches ✓. Here the OH group does not stay together (its atoms end up in water), so we count O and H separately.

### Worked example 6: sodium hydroxide and sulfuric acid

Sodium hydroxide reacts with sulfuric acid to make sodium sulfate and water. SO₄ appears on both sides, so treat it as one unit.

$$\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + \text{H}_2\text{O}$$

1. Count, with SO₄ as one unit. Left: Na 1, SO₄ 1, H $1 + 2 = 3$, O outside SO₄ 1. Right: Na 2, SO₄ 1, H 2, O outside SO₄ 1.
2. Na: 1 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 2 in front of NaOH. The left now has H $2 + 2 = 4$ and 2 O outside SO₄.
3. SO₄: 1 on each side. It matches.
4. H: 4 on the left, 2 on the right. Put 2 in front of H₂O. The right now has 4 H and 2 O.
5. Final equation:

$$2\text{NaOH} + \text{H}_2\text{SO}_4 \rightarrow \text{Na}_2\text{SO}_4 + 2\text{H}_2\text{O}$$

| Atom or group | Left (start) | Right (start) | Left (end) | Right (end) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Na | 1 | 2 | 2 | 2 |
| SO₄ | 1 | 1 | 1 | 1 |
| H | 3 | 2 | 4 | 4 |
| O outside SO₄ | 1 | 1 | 2 | 2 |

Check with every atom counted: O on the left is $2 + 4 = 6$ and on the right $4 + 2 = 6$; Na 2 = 2, S 1 = 1, H 4 = 4 ✓

## State symbols

We often add the state of each substance: (s) solid, (l) liquid, (g) gas, (aq) dissolved in water. State symbols do not change how you balance.

$$\text{Zn(s)} + 2\text{HCl(aq)} \rightarrow \text{ZnCl}_2\text{(aq)} + \text{H}_2\text{(g)}$$

$$2\text{H}_2\text{(g)} + \text{O}_2\text{(g)} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O(l)}$$

## Common mistakes

- **Changing a subscript.** Writing H₂ + O₂ → H₂O₂ makes the atoms match, but H₂O₂ is hydrogen peroxide, a different substance. Only change coefficients.
- **Putting a coefficient inside a formula.** Write 2H₂O, never H₂2O. The coefficient always goes in front of the whole formula.
- **Forgetting that a coefficient multiplies every atom.** 2H₂O has 4 H **and** 2 O, not 4 H and 1 O.
- **Forgetting to multiply the bracket.** Ca(OH)₂ has 2 O and 2 H, not 1 of each.
- **Not recounting.** After each change, recount every element. For example, 2Mg + 2O₂ → 2MgO looks tidy, but it has 4 O on the left and 2 O on the right.
- **Not using the smallest numbers.** 4H₂ + 2O₂ → 4H₂O is balanced, but every coefficient can be divided by 2. Write 2H₂ + O₂ → 2H₂O.
