## What is electrolysis?

**Electrolysis** is the breaking down (decomposition) of a compound by passing a **direct current** (d.c.) of electricity through it when it is **molten** or **dissolved in water**.

Key words:

- **Electrolyte**: a compound that conducts electricity when molten or in solution and is decomposed by it. Examples: molten sodium chloride, copper(II) sulfate solution, dilute sulfuric acid. It conducts because its **ions are free to move**.
- **Non-electrolyte**: a substance that does not conduct, such as sugar solution or ethanol. It is made of molecules, not ions.
- **Electrodes**: rods that carry the current into and out of the electrolyte. **Inert** electrodes, such as graphite (carbon) or platinum, do not react.
- **Anode**: the **positive** electrode. It attracts negative ions (**anions**).
- **Cathode**: the **negative** electrode. It attracts positive ions (**cations**).

A solid ionic compound does **not** conduct, because its ions are held in fixed positions. Melting it or dissolving it lets the ions move.

## What happens at the electrodes

- At the **cathode**, positive ions **gain** electrons. This is **reduction**.
- At the **anode**, negative ions **lose** electrons. This is **oxidation**.

We show these changes with **half-equations**. The number of electrons lost at the anode always equals the number gained at the cathode.

## Worked example 1: molten lead(II) bromide

Lead(II) bromide, PbBr₂, is heated until it melts and is electrolysed with graphite electrodes. Predict the products.

**Step 1: ions present.** Pb²⁺ and Br⁻.

**Step 2: cathode (−).** Pb²⁺ ions are attracted and gain electrons:
$$\text{Pb}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Pb}$$
Shiny molten lead collects under the cathode.

**Step 3: anode (+).** Br⁻ ions are attracted and lose electrons:
$$2\text{Br}^- \rightarrow \text{Br}_2 + 2e^-$$
A red-brown vapour of bromine is seen.

**Step 4: check and combine.** 2 electrons are gained and 2 are lost, so:
$$\text{PbBr}_2(l) \rightarrow \text{Pb}(l) + \text{Br}_2(g)$$

For any **molten** ionic compound of one metal and one non-metal, the **metal** forms at the cathode and the **non-metal** at the anode.

## Electrolysis of solutions

Water itself contains a few hydrogen ions and hydroxide ions:
$$\text{H}_2\text{O}(l) \rightleftharpoons \text{H}^+(aq) + \text{OH}^-(aq)$$
So in a solution, two kinds of ion compete at each electrode.

**At the cathode:** the metal is deposited if it is **less reactive than hydrogen** (for example copper or silver). If the metal is more reactive (sodium, potassium, calcium, magnesium), **hydrogen** forms instead:
$$2\text{H}^+ + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{H}_2$$

**At the anode (inert electrodes):** chlorine, bromine or iodine forms from a **concentrated** solution of a halide. Otherwise **oxygen** forms from hydroxide ions; sulfate and nitrate ions are not discharged:
$$4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4e^-$$

| Electrolyte (inert electrodes) | Cathode product | Anode product | What is left |
|---|---|---|---|
| Molten sodium chloride | sodium | chlorine | nothing |
| Concentrated sodium chloride solution (brine) | hydrogen | chlorine | sodium hydroxide solution |
| Dilute sulfuric acid | hydrogen | oxygen | more concentrated acid |
| Copper(II) sulfate solution | copper | oxygen | sulfuric acid; the blue colour fades |

Tests: hydrogen burns with a squeaky **pop** when a lighted splint is held near it; oxygen **relights a glowing splint**; chlorine **bleaches** damp blue litmus paper (it may turn red first).

## Worked example 2: electrolysis of dilute sulfuric acid

Dilute sulfuric acid is electrolysed with graphite electrodes, and 36 cm³ of gas is collected at the cathode. What gas is it, and what volume of gas is collected at the anode?

- Cathode: $4\text{H}^+ + 4e^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2$, so the gas is **hydrogen**.
- Anode: $4\text{OH}^- \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2\text{O} + \text{O}_2 + 4e^-$, so the gas is **oxygen**.
- The same 4 electrons make 2 molecules of H₂ but only 1 molecule of O₂. At the same temperature and pressure, equal volumes of gases contain equal numbers of molecules, so the volumes are in the ratio $2 : 1$.
- Volume of oxygen $= 36 \div 2 = 18$ cm³.

Overall, water is being split: $2\text{H}_2\text{O} \rightarrow 2\text{H}_2 + \text{O}_2$.

## How much is deposited?

The amount of electric charge $Q$ (in coulombs, C) passed is

$$Q = I \times t$$

where $I$ is the current in amperes and $t$ is the time in **seconds**. One mole of electrons carries **96 500 C**, called the **Faraday constant** ($F$).

## Worked example 3: copper plating

A current of 2.0 A flows through copper(II) sulfate solution for 32 minutes 10 seconds. What mass of copper is deposited on the cathode? (Cu = 64)

- Time: $t = 32 \times 60 + 10 = 1920 + 10 = 1930$ s
- Charge: $Q = 2.0 \times 1930 = 3860$ C
- Moles of electrons $= \dfrac{3860}{96\,500} = 0.040$ mol
- Half-equation: $\text{Cu}^{2+} + 2e^- \rightarrow \text{Cu}$, so 2 mol of electrons give 1 mol of copper
- Moles of copper $= 0.040 \div 2 = 0.020$ mol
- Mass of copper $= 0.020 \times 64 = 1.28$ g

## Uses of electrolysis

- **Electroplating** covers an object with a thin layer of metal to protect it or make it look attractive. The object is the **cathode**, the anode is the plating metal, and the electrolyte contains ions of that metal. Chromium-plated handlebars on moto taxis resist rust, and steel food cans are plated with tin, a metal whose ore (cassiterite) is mined in Rwanda.
- **Purifying copper**: impure copper is the anode and pure copper the cathode, in copper(II) sulfate solution. Copper dissolves from the anode and is deposited on the cathode. Impurities fall to the bottom.
- **Extracting reactive metals** such as sodium and aluminium from their molten compounds.
- **Electrolysis of brine** makes chlorine (used to make drinking water safe and to make bleach), hydrogen, and sodium hydroxide (used to make soap).

## Safety in the lab

Use only **low-voltage d.c.** from batteries or a power pack (about 6 V), never mains electricity. Chlorine and bromine are **toxic**: collect only small volumes and work in a well-ventilated room. Molten lead(II) bromide must be a teacher demonstration in a fume cupboard because lead compounds and bromine are poisonous. Wear goggles, keep the electrodes apart so they do not touch, and wash your hands after handling copper(II) sulfate.
