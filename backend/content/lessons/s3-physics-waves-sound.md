## What is a wave?

Drop a stone into calm water at the edge of Lake Kivu and ripples spread out in circles. A leaf floating on the water bobs **up and down**, but it is not carried away to the shore.

A **wave** is a disturbance that transfers **energy** from one place to another **without transferring matter**.

## Two kinds of wave

| | Transverse wave | Longitudinal wave |
|---|---|---|
| Vibration of particles | at right angles (perpendicular) to the direction the wave travels | parallel to (along) the direction the wave travels |
| Made of | crests and troughs | compressions and rarefactions |
| Examples | water ripples, waves on a rope, light | sound, a pushed-and-pulled slinky spring |

Waves that need a material (a **medium**) to travel through, such as water waves and sound, are **mechanical waves**. Light and radio waves are **electromagnetic waves**; they can travel through a vacuum.

## Describing a wave

- **Amplitude ($A$):** the greatest displacement of a particle from its rest position (in m).
- **Wavelength ($\lambda$):** the distance between two neighbouring crests, or two neighbouring compressions (in m).
- **Frequency ($f$):** the number of complete waves passing a point each second, in **hertz (Hz)**. 1 Hz = 1 wave per second.
- **Period ($T$):** the time for one complete wave to pass, in seconds.

Frequency and period are linked by

$$T = \frac{1}{f}$$

## The wave equation

In one period the wave moves forward one wavelength, so speed = distance ÷ time = $\lambda \div T$. Since $\frac{1}{T} = f$:

$$v = f\lambda$$

where $v$ is the wave speed in m/s.

### Worked example 1: waves at Rubavu beach

At Rubavu beach, a student counts 30 waves reaching a post in 60 s. The distance between neighbouring crests is 2.5 m.

**Frequency:** $f = \dfrac{30}{60} = 0.5$ Hz

**Period:** $T = \dfrac{1}{f} = \dfrac{1}{0.5} = 2$ s

**Speed:** $v = f\lambda = 0.5 \times 2.5 = 1.25$ m/s

## Sound waves

Sound is made by **vibrating objects**: the skin of an *ingoma* drum, the strings of an *inanga*, or your vocal cords. The vibrating object pushes and pulls on the air, making a **longitudinal** wave of compressions and rarefactions.

Sound is a mechanical wave, so it **needs a medium**. If an electric bell rings inside a glass jar and the air is pumped out, the sound fades away, even though you can still see the hammer hitting the bell. Sound cannot travel through a vacuum.

Sound travels faster in solids and liquids than in gases, because the particles are closer together and more strongly linked, so they pass on the vibrations more quickly:

| Medium | Approximate speed of sound |
|---|---|
| Air | 340 m/s |
| Water | 1500 m/s |
| Steel | 5000 to 6000 m/s |

### Worked example 2: a drum note

An *ingoma* drum produces a note of frequency 256 Hz. Find the wavelength of the sound in air (speed 340 m/s).

$$\lambda = \frac{v}{f} = \frac{340}{256} \approx 1.33\ \text{m}$$

## Pitch, loudness and hearing

- **Pitch** depends on **frequency**: a higher frequency gives a higher note.
- **Loudness** depends on **amplitude**: a larger amplitude gives a louder sound.

Healthy young people hear frequencies from about **20 Hz to 20 000 Hz**. Sound above 20 000 Hz is **ultrasound**; it is used in hospital scans and by bats. Sound below 20 Hz is **infrasound**; elephants in Akagera National Park use it to communicate over long distances.

## Echoes

An **echo** is a sound **reflected** from a hard surface such as a cliff, a hillside or a tall building. The sound travels to the surface **and back**, so

$$d = \frac{v \times t}{2}$$

where $d$ is the distance to the surface and $t$ is the time between the sound and its echo.

### Worked example 3: an echo from a quarry face

A student near a quarry face in Huye claps and hears the echo 1.5 s later. Take the speed of sound in air as 340 m/s.

$$d = \frac{340 \times 1.5}{2} = \frac{510}{2} = 255\ \text{m}$$

### Worked example 4: measuring Lake Kivu with sonar

A research boat on Lake Kivu sends a pulse of ultrasound straight down. The echo from the lake bed returns after 0.4 s. Sound travels at 1500 m/s in water.

$$d = \frac{1500 \times 0.4}{2} = \frac{600}{2} = 300\ \text{m}$$

The water is 300 m deep at that point. This method is called **echo sounding** (or sonar).

## Thunder and lightning

Light travels so fast (300 000 000 m/s) that we see a lightning flash almost instantly. The thunder takes longer. If you hear thunder 3 s after the flash, the storm is about $340 \times 3 = 1020$ m away, roughly 1 km. Rwanda has many thunderstorms, so when the gap is short, go indoors at once and stay away from tall isolated trees and open fields.

## Safety in the laboratory

- When using a signal generator and loudspeaker, start at a **low volume**. Very loud sounds, especially high-pitched ones, can damage your hearing.
- A ripple tank holds water near an electric lamp and motor. Keep mains plugs and sockets well away from the water, and wipe up spills straight away.
- When timing echoes outdoors, stay well back from the edge of any cliff or quarry face.
