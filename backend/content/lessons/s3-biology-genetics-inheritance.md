## Why do offspring look like their parents?

A calf often has the same coat colour as its mother. Children often share features with their parents. **Genetics** is the study of how characteristics are passed from parents to offspring. This passing on is called **inheritance**.

## Chromosomes, genes and alleles

- The **nucleus** of each cell contains thread-like **chromosomes**, made of a chemical called **DNA**.
- Human body cells have **46 chromosomes**, arranged in **23 pairs**. One chromosome of each pair came from the mother and one from the father.
- Sex cells (gametes: sperm and eggs) have only **23 chromosomes**, one from each pair. At fertilisation the number returns to 46.
- A **gene** is a short section of DNA that controls one characteristic, such as horns in cattle.
- A gene can exist in different forms, called **alleles**. For example, the gene for horns in cattle has a "hornless" allele and a "horned" allele.

Because chromosomes come in pairs, every individual carries **two alleles** of each gene.

## Key words

| Term | Meaning | Example (cattle horns) |
|---|---|---|
| Dominant allele | Shows its effect even if only one copy is present; written as a capital letter | P = polled (hornless) |
| Recessive allele | Shows its effect only when two copies are present; written as a small letter | p = horned |
| Genotype | The pair of alleles an organism has | PP, Pp or pp |
| Phenotype | The characteristic that can be seen or measured | Polled or horned |
| Homozygous | Two identical alleles | PP or pp |
| Heterozygous | Two different alleles | Pp |

In cattle the polled (hornless) allele really is dominant to the horned allele. A cow with genotype **Pp** is polled, because one P allele is enough.

## Mendel's experiments

In the 1860s the monk **Gregor Mendel** crossed pea plants and counted their offspring. When he let hybrid plants, each carrying one "round" allele and one hidden "wrinkled" allele, pollinate themselves, he got 5474 plants with round seeds and 1850 with wrinkled seeds:

$$\frac{5474}{1850} \approx 2.96$$

This is very close to a **3 : 1** ratio, which is exactly what a Punnett square predicts.

## Genetic crosses with a Punnett square

Follow these steps:

1. Choose letters for the alleles and write the parents' genotypes.
2. Write the possible gametes of each parent (each gamete carries **one** allele).
3. Draw a Punnett square and combine the gametes.
4. State the genotypes and phenotypes of the offspring, and their ratio.

### Worked example 1: a pure-breeding cross

A farmer in Nyagatare crosses a homozygous polled bull (PP) with a horned cow (pp).

- Gametes: the bull can only give **P**; the cow can only give **p**.
- Every calf gets P from the bull and p from the cow: genotype **Pp**.
- Phenotype: **all polled**, because P is dominant.

### Worked example 2: crossing two heterozygous cattle

Two of these Pp calves grow up and are crossed: Pp × Pp. Each parent makes two kinds of gamete, P and p, in equal numbers.

| | **P** | **p** |
|---|---|---|
| **P** | PP | Pp |
| **p** | Pp | pp |

- Genotype ratio: 1 PP : 2 Pp : 1 pp
- Phenotypes: PP and Pp are polled; pp is horned
- Phenotype ratio: **3 polled : 1 horned**
- The chance that a calf is horned is $\frac{1}{4} = 25\%$.

If such parents produce 12 calves over several years, we expect $\frac{3}{4} \times 12 = 9$ polled and $\frac{1}{4} \times 12 = 3$ horned. Real numbers may differ a little, because which sperm fertilises which egg happens by chance.

### Worked example 3: the test cross

A polled bull could be PP or Pp. To find out, cross him with horned (pp) cows. If he is Pp:

| | **P** | **p** |
|---|---|---|
| **p** | Pp | pp |

Half the calves ($\frac{1}{2} = 50\%$) are expected to be horned. If even **one** horned calf is born, the bull must carry p, so he is **Pp**. If he is PP, all the calves are polled.

## Boy or girl?

Sex is decided by one pair of chromosomes: females are **XX** and males are **XY**.

| | **X** (from sperm) | **Y** (from sperm) |
|---|---|---|
| **X** (from egg) | XX girl | XY boy |

All eggs carry an X. Half the sperm carry X and half carry Y, so at every fertilisation the chance of a boy is $\frac{1}{2}$, and the chance of a girl is $\frac{1}{2}$.

A family in Rubavu already has three daughters. The chance that their next baby is a boy is still $\frac{1}{2}$: each fertilisation is a new event, and earlier births do not change the odds.

## Variation

Individuals of the same species differ. This is **variation**.

- **Discontinuous variation** falls into clear groups with nothing in between: ABO blood group, polled or horned cattle, male or female. It is usually controlled by one or a few genes.
- **Continuous variation** has a full range of values: height, body mass, milk yield of cows. It is controlled by many genes **and** affected by the environment, such as diet.

New alleles arise by **mutation**, a random change in a gene. Radiation (for example X-rays) and some chemicals increase the rate of mutation.
